# Discovery interview — Station Manager (busy interchange)

Scenario probes removed; follow-ups chosen by the persona.

**Simulated by:** Claude subagent (discovery method, no system described) · **Date:** 2026-10-06 · **Persona:** Station manager of a busy interchange; runs platforms, gates, information staff, crowd safety and facilities; does not decide whether trains run.

## Who I am
I run a big interchange station. I am responsible for the platforms, the gates, the information staff, crowd safety and the facilities. I am on the concourse as much as in the office, and rush hour is the job. I do not decide whether trains run; the control office does, and I live with their decisions.

## Answers

### Q1. A normal day
I am in before the first trains, walking the platforms and checking that gates, lifts, toilets and lighting work and that cleaners have been round. Then a briefing with the duty staff about engineering works and events. Morning peak is mostly standing on the concourse watching crowd density at the stairs and gates. Between peaks I handle staff rosters, complaints, contractor visits and incident paperwork. The evening peak is the same as the morning, plus more drunks and events. Throughout, my radio and phone are never off, and I keep one eye on the boards.

### Q2. What breaks
Screens die, gates jam, a lift fails, a platform floods, a train is held in the platform and blocks the flow. I send an engineer or a gateline team and put a staff member there with a high-vis and a loudhailer. For a failed lift I redirect people and tell staff to help. The worst breaks are the ones I hear about late, from a passenger or a staff member, not from any system. By then there is a queue and people are angry.

### Q3. Information I rely on
I rely on the control office, by phone and radio, for what the trains are doing. I rely on my own staff for what is physically happening on the platforms. I look at the same boards passengers do, which is a bit embarrassing. I need platform and delay changes as early as possible, ideally minutes before the public sees them, because I need time to move staff. For equipment faults I rely on staff reports and contractor calls. I am guessing here, but I think the information I get is often only as good as the last person who phoned.

### Q4. What must never happen
Never a crowd crush on a platform or stairs. Never passengers on the track side because they were told the wrong platform. Never staff telling people something different from the boards. Never a blocked evacuation route. I also never want to find out from a passenger that something has been wrong for an hour.

### Q5. If the information were confidently wrong
I trust my eyes first. If the board says platform 4 and I see the train arriving at 6, I tell staff to announce it manually and I call control to confirm. Confident wrongness is worse than a blank screen, because people follow it. If I could not confirm quickly, I would assume the safe option, hold people back from the platform edge and use the loudhailer. Afterwards I would want to know how long it was wrong and who saw it.

## Follow-ups (chosen by me)

### Follow-up 1. Hearing about a late platform change
Last evening peak (details are from memory, I'm guessing at the exact minutes): I first noticed a gateline officer pointing at the board, which had flipped platform 4 to 6. No call yet. I radioed control and got "yes, changed a few minutes ago". Meanwhile about two hundred people were on the wrong stairs. I pulled four staff off the concourse with loudhailers; it took maybe five minutes to turn the crowd. Some people ran, one fell on the stairs, and a few missed the train. It ended with no serious injury, but I found out from my own staff, not from anyone telling me.

### Follow-up 2. A screen showing wrong or dead information
A few weeks back, a screen on the footbridge showed a train that had left twenty minutes earlier. A passenger told me at the information desk; I'm guessing it had been like that for over an hour. I walked up, compared it with the next board, then phoned the contractor, who took most of the afternoon. I put a staff member beside it. Several people had already waited for a train that was gone, and I had two complaints. It ended with the screen swapped, no record of how long it was wrong, and nobody I could say it to except the contractor.

## Extracted by the persona
- **What I rely on today**: control office by phone and radio; my own staff; what I can see; the public boards; contractors for faults.
- **What is hardest or most error-prone in my work**: hearing about changes and faults late, usually from my own staff or passengers; crowd build-up when information is slow; keeping staff, announcements and boards consistent.
- **What must never happen**: a crush; passengers sent to the wrong platform; a blocked evacuation route; staff contradicting the boards.
- **What would make me stop trusting information**: one visible mismatch between a board and the actual train; stale times; an item I cannot trace to anyone.
- **What nobody gives me today that I would want**: early warning of changes before the public sees them; knowing a screen is dead or stale without someone telling me; a record of how long something was wrong.
