# Discovery interview — Customer-service representative

Scenario probes removed; follow-ups chosen by the persona.

**Simulated by:** Claude subagent (discovery method, no system described) · **Date:** 2026-10-06 · **Persona:** Call-centre customer-service representative at a national railway, handling calls and messages, often during disruption.

## Who I am
I answer passenger calls, emails and social messages for the railway. I have no control over trains, platforms or refunds beyond what policy lets me do. Most contacts are routine, but during disruption the queue explodes and people are angry. My job is to give an accurate answer, quickly, and to stay calm. I'm guessing at some details of how the wider organisation works.

## Answers

### Q1. A normal day
I log in, check the shift briefing, and read any notes on known problems. Then it's a steady stream: ticket questions, delay compensation, lost property, accessibility assistance, complaints. A quiet day is mostly questions about fares and refunds. On a bad day, one incident turns every call into the same call. I spend a lot of time looking things up in several places while the caller waits. I log each contact with a reason code and a short note.

### Q2. What breaks
When something major happens, the briefing is already out of date and nobody has told us what passengers are hearing. Callers know more than I do, from apps or social media. I ask a supervisor, who asks the control room, and that takes minutes the caller doesn't have. I fall back on a holding line: I apologise, explain what I can see, and give the official refund route. I also log a note so the next agent doesn't start from zero. The worst failures are conflicting messages: the board, the app and my screen all say different things.

### Q3. Information I rely on
I rely on our internal service-status screen, the timetable system, the ticketing system for the booking, supervisor updates, and honestly the same public sources passengers use. I need it the moment the caller gives me a train, because the call is live. I need to know whether the train is running, delayed or cancelled, what was published, and when it was published. The "when" matters most. Without it I can't tell whether the caller saw something stale or I'm looking at something stale.

### Q4. Never see, never happen
I must never see another person's payment details beyond what's needed to confirm identity, or full card numbers. I must never be able to change a train's status, cancel a service or issue a refund outside the rules. Staff-only operational detail, such as crew names, security matters or exact incident causes before release, should not be in front of me, because I'd repeat it. I must never promise something the railway hasn't committed to.

### Q5. If the information were confidently wrong
I would not know unless a passenger told me, and that's the danger. I'd repeat it and it would sound authoritative. Once I spotted a mismatch, I'd stop quoting it, tell the caller what I can verify, and escalate to my supervisor. I would also log it so others see the pattern. I'd be far more cautious if I couldn't see where the information came from or how old it was.

## Follow-ups

### Follow-up 1. Conflicting status during a major disruption (costs trust and time)
Last bad one, I'm guessing at the exact date, a morning signalling fault. First thing I saw was my queue jump from about five waiting to forty. The first caller said the app showed their train "on time" while the station announcement said cancelled. My status screen showed "delayed". I asked my team leader, who rang the control room and waited roughly ten minutes. Meanwhile I gave four callers the holding line. Then the answer came back: cancelled, and the earlier "delayed" entry had never been updated. Two callers had already been told "delayed" by me and went to the station. One complained, and I logged it as my error, though the screen was wrong. It ended with a corrected briefing about twenty minutes after the first call.

### Follow-up 2. Refund refused because I couldn't prove the cancellation (costs money and trust)
A passenger rang three days after a cancelled evening service wanting a full refund. First I pulled the booking: a promotional fare bought through a third party, so I couldn't see the purchase details. I then searched our status screen for that train and date and found nothing, as old entries drop off the front view. I asked a colleague, then a supervisor, about fifteen minutes in total, and nobody could find the cancellation record. Policy says no proof, no refund, so I declined. The passenger got angry, put it on social media, and escalated. A week later, I'm guessing, a manager approved the refund after finding the record. It cost the passenger a wasted call, cost us a complaint handler's time, and cost the refund anyway.

## Extracted by the persona
- **What I rely on today**: internal status screen, timetable and ticketing systems, shift briefings, supervisors, public apps and social media.
- **What is hardest or most error-prone in my work**: finding out what passengers were told, and when; conflicting messages across channels; **status entries that are never corrected; cancellation records that vanish from view after a day or two**; unclear refund rules by ticket type.
- **What must never happen**: being able to change operational status or refunds beyond limits; seeing staff-only or payment data; repeating unreleased causes; promising what isn't committed.
- **What would make me stop trusting information**: no timestamp or source; contradiction between board, app and my screen; passengers repeatedly reporting the opposite.
- **What nobody gives me today that I would want**: a single view of what each channel told passengers and when, flagged as stale or disputed; **a searchable history of past cancellations I can cite on a refund decision**.
