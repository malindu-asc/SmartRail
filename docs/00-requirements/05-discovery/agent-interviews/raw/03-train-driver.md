# Discovery interview — Train driver

Scenario probes removed; follow-ups chosen by the persona.

**Simulated by:** Claude subagent (discovery method, no system described) · **Date:** 2026-10-06 · **Persona:** Train driver, fifteen years on the same route, national railway; safety-critical comms by railway radio.

## Who I am
I drive passenger trains and have done the same route for fifteen years. I know every signal, gradient and bad patch of track on it. My hands are in gloves, the cab shakes, and low sun can blind me. My eyes are on the line, so I can't read or type while moving. I'm wary of being blamed for delays that weren't mine, and of being tracked.

## Answers
### Q1 Normal day
I sign on, collect my duty and any notices about speed restrictions or works, and check the train and its brake test. I take the train out and drive to the timetable, watching signals, speed boards and the line. At stations I stop, watch the doors and the platform, and wait for the dispatch signal. Between stops I'm mostly just driving and listening to the radio. At the end I hand over, and I fill in a report if anything odd happened. Most days nothing happens, and that is the aim.

### Q2 What breaks
Signals fail, trains ahead are late, doors fault, and the train itself sometimes loses traction or brakes. Weather causes a lot: leaves, ice, flooding. When something breaks I stop safely and tell the signaller on the radio. I follow their instructions, and the rule book covers most cases. If I need technical help, control or the fleet people talk me through it. I write it up afterwards.

### Q3 Information I rely on
I rely on the signals, the line-side signs and the notices I'm given at sign-on. I also rely on the radio for anything that changes. I need it before I start and at once if it changes. If a speed restriction isn't on my sheet, that worries me. I'm guessing at the exact paperwork; it varies between companies.

### Q4 Never see / never happen
I must never be distracted by a screen or message while driving. I must never get conflicting instructions, such as the signal saying one thing and someone else another. Never a wrong speed restriction. Also never being blamed on the record for a delay caused by others, and never being tracked like a parcel for disciplinary reasons.

### Q5 Confidently wrong information
The signal and the signaller win over anything else, and I stop and ask on the radio. If a notice is wrong, for example a restriction that doesn't exist or one that is missing, I go by what I see and what the signaller says. I'd report it afterwards. I'd trust that source less for a good while.

## Follow-ups
### Follow-up 1 — A speed restriction missing from my sheet
The last time, I'm guessing it was a few months back, I came round a bend and saw the temporary speed board at the line side. It wasn't on my sheet from sign-on. I slowed to the board's speed straight away; that is the rule, the board wins. At the next station I rang control on the radio and asked whether it was a new restriction. They hadn't told anyone on the earlier trains either; the notice had been issued after my sheet was printed. That took about ten minutes of calls while I was holding the platform. Nothing bad happened because I saw the board in time and knew the bend. If it had been a dark winter morning, I'm guessing I could have been too fast. Control told the following drivers by radio and I wrote a report. Nobody ever told me whether the sheet was corrected.

### Follow-up 2 — Being blamed for a delay that wasn't mine
Last autumn I lost about twelve minutes at a station because the platform was occupied by the train in front, and the signal stayed red. I waited, rang the signaller to ask, and was told to stand. Then leaves made the rails slippery on the next climb, so I braked early and lost another few minutes. Later I was asked why I was late. I had to explain from memory, since my own notes were only what I'd written. I couldn't prove the red signal was held; I'm guessing there's a record somewhere, but I never saw it. In the end it was marked as mine in part, I think, until a supervisor checked. It left me feeling I had to defend myself every time.
