# Discovery interview — Accessibility specialist

Scenario probes removed; follow-ups chosen by the persona.

**Simulated by:** Claude subagent (discovery method, no system described) · **Date:** 2026-10-06 · **Persona:** Accessibility specialist with public transport experience, advocating for passengers with low vision, blindness, limited mobility, hearing loss, cognitive differences and limited language.

## Who I am
I have spent years auditing stations, vehicles and passenger information for access. I work with disabled passenger groups and with operators' staff. I know WCAG, but I mostly talk about what happens to a real person at a real platform at 7:40 on a wet Tuesday.

## Answers

### Q1
A normal day is a mix of audits, complaint review and advising teams. I walk a station as different passengers would: with a cane, in a wheelchair, with a hearing loop switched on, with a suitcase and a toddler. I read the complaints that came in, and most are not about big failures. They are things like "the announcement was too quick" or "the lift was out and nobody told me". I sit in design and procurement meetings and ask whether anyone has tested this with real users. Often the answer is no. I also train station staff on assisting people, because technology never covers everything.

### Q2
What breaks is usually the lift, the hearing loop, the ramp, or a screen that is dead or washed out by sun. When a passenger meets a broken thing, they need to know before they commit to the journey, not at the stairs. Today staff improvise: they carry a wheelchair user, redirect them, or phone ahead. That works only if staff know in time, and often they don't. I am guessing at the exact internal processes, but from the passenger side the pattern is that the fault is known somewhere and the passenger is the last to hear.

### Q3
I rely on station and fleet access data: which stations are step-free, which lifts work, where the assistance points are. I get it from audits, operator staff and passengers themselves. Passengers need it before they leave home and again at the station, and they need it in more than one form: visual, audio, large print, plain words. Honestly, much of it today is out of date or buried in a PDF. I need to know when it was last confirmed.

### Q4
Nobody should be stranded on a platform they cannot leave. Nobody should get critical information only by colour, only by sound, or only in a tiny font. A passenger must never be told a route is accessible when it isn't, because a wrong "yes" is worse than "I don't know". No one should be required to ask for help in public in order to find out basic facts. Alerts that flash or move so fast people can't read them are also a problem.

### Q5
If the information were confidently wrong, the passenger would act on it and be harmed: they would go to the lift that is out of order and be stuck. A person with low vision or a screen reader cannot easily cross-check, so confident errors hit them hardest. Staff would have to catch it by luck. I would want wrong or stale information to say that it is uncertain, plainly, in words, not by a subtle grey tint. Passengers then fall back on staff or ask someone.

### Follow-up 1 — The lift is out and the passenger finds out at the stairs
Last time, a wheelchair user on one of our passenger groups wrote to me, upset. She had travelled to a mid-size station and found the lift to her platform out of order. First she saw a paper sign taped to the lift door, no alternative given. She looked for staff; the one person on duty was in the ticket office behind glass and took several minutes to come. He phoned the next station to ask whether its lift worked, which took maybe 15 minutes in total, I'm guessing from her account. Then a taxi was arranged to the other station. She missed her connection. It ended with an apology and a refund request, but she told me she would not travel alone there again. What went wrong was that the fault had been logged hours earlier, I'm guessing, but nobody told her before she left.

### Follow-up 2 — A published "step-free" claim that was no longer true
The last time was during an audit. A leaflet and a web page both listed a station as step-free. I walked it and found a temporary barrier from building works forcing a detour over steps. I checked the web page's source, then called the operator's access contact. It took two phone calls and about a day to find who owned that entry. They confirmed the works had started weeks earlier; nobody had updated the listing. They corrected the web page but I'm guessing the printed leaflet stayed in circulation. A passenger group member had already planned a trip from it. It ended with a correction, but trust took the hit: the group now double-checks everything by phone.

## Extracted by the persona
- **What I rely on today**: audits, operator staff and access contacts, passenger complaints and groups, access data about stations and equipment (often outdated); phone calls to check.
- **What is hardest or most error-prone in my work**: knowing the live state of lifts, ramps and loops; getting that to the passenger before they commit; finding who owns a fact and keeping printed and online copies consistent.
- **What must never happen**: telling someone a route is accessible when it isn't; critical information by only one sense; a passenger stranded; a fault known internally but not told to the passenger.
- **What would make me stop trusting information**: stale data with no date; a confident "accessible" that proved wrong; listings that differ between leaflet, web and announcement.
- **What nobody gives me today that I would want**: a plain statement of how fresh and certain each access fact is; early warning of an access fault before the passenger travels; one named owner per fact.
- **Confidence**: Medium. I am confident on passenger needs and access principles, but I am guessing at this railway's internal systems and timings.
