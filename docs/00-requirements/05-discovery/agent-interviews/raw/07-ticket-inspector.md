# Discovery interview — Ticket inspector (on board)

Scenario probes removed; follow-ups chosen by the persona.

**Simulated by:** Claude subagent (discovery method, no system described) · **Date:** 2026-10-06 · **Persona:** Ticket inspector on moving national-railway trains; one hand free, crowded carriages, patchy signal, needs a fast answer with a reason I can explain politely.

## Who I am
I work through the train checking tickets, often in a crowded carriage with one hand holding on. I have a few seconds per passenger and a queue behind me. Signal comes and goes, especially in cuttings and rural stretches. I'm guessing at some details of how my employer's systems work; this is how it feels from the aisle.

## Answers

### Q1. A normal day
I sign on, check which train and which stretch I'm covering, and board. I start at one end and work through, passenger by passenger, looking at the ticket, checking date, class and where it's valid between. At busy times I only get to some people, so I pick the ones who look unsure or who boarded late. I also deal with people who have no ticket and need to buy one, and with questions about connections and platforms. At the end I write up any penalty fares or incidents.

### Q2. What breaks
Signal drops, so anything that needs a lookup stalls. Phones are cracked, run flat, or the ticket will not display. Printed tickets are crumpled or unreadable. Trains get cancelled or changed, and then I have passengers holding tickets for a service that was not what they expected, and I'm not always told. When something fails I fall back on judgement and the paper or screen in front of me. If in doubt I tend to let it go and note it, because arguing in a packed carriage causes more trouble than one fare.

### Q3. Information I rely on
I rely on the ticket itself: date, route, class, validity. I rely on what I know of the day's timetable and any disruption I've been told about, mostly by the guard, control, or a radio message. I need it before I reach the passenger, not after. If a ticket is valid because of a disruption, I need to know that rule is in force today, on this train. I don't always have that, and I mostly find out from passengers telling me.

### Q4. What I must never see or do
I must never accuse an honest passenger of fare evasion, especially after a disruption that was not their fault. I must never penalise someone wrongly and then have it overturned. I must never miss genuine misuse because I trusted a bad answer. I must never have to say "the computer says so" without a reason I can give. It also must never slow me so much that the carriage backs up or I become a safety problem in the aisle.

### Q5. If the information were confidently wrong
I'd rely on my own eyes and judgement and not on a confident answer I couldn't explain. If it says "invalid" and the ticket looks fine, I'd not penalise; I'd note it and move on. If it says "valid" and something looks off, such as the wrong date or a clearly altered ticket, I'd still query it. Once I've been burned a couple of times I stop trusting it altogether and revert to paper habits. I'd want to be able to say that the information was wrong without it counting against me.

## Follow-ups

### Follow-up 1. Not being told about a disruption (cost: trust and time)
The last time, I'm guessing it was a few weeks back, I boarded and the first three passengers showed tickets for a train earlier than mine. I saw that first and assumed it was misuse. The fourth said their train had been cancelled and they'd been put on this one. Nobody had told me. I radioed the guard, who didn't know either, so I rang control from the vestibule, which took maybe ten minutes with the signal in and out. Control said tickets for the cancelled service were being accepted. By then I'd turned away one family at the door and told them to pay, and they'd already got angry. I went back, apologised and let them stay. I wrote it up. It ended with no penalty, but I'd lost the first stretch of the carriage and my credibility with them.

### Follow-up 2. Losing signal mid-check (cost: time and safety)
Last time, I'm guessing a fortnight ago, I was halfway down a packed carriage when the signal went in a cutting. I saw the ticket fail to load on a passenger's phone and a queue forming behind me. I couldn't check anything beyond what I could see: date and route on the screen. I asked the passenger to wait, but they were standing and the train was swaying, so I let them sit and moved on. Around eight people I couldn't properly check. I noted their seats on my hand and went back after about fifteen minutes when signal returned. Two had got off by then. One of those I'm guessing was genuinely wrong. It ended with me not knowing how many I'd missed.

## Extracted by the persona
- **What I rely on today**: the ticket (screen or paper), my knowledge of the timetable, radio or calls with guard and control, what passengers tell me, my own judgement.
- **What is hardest or most error-prone in my work**: not being told about cancellations or changes until a passenger mentions them; losing signal mid-check; telling honest people from chancers; time pressure in crowds.
- **What must never happen**: wrongly penalising or turning away an honest passenger; being unable to explain a decision; missing real misuse; holding up the carriage; unsafe handling in a moving train.
- **What would make me stop trusting information**: being confidently wrong a couple of times in front of a passenger; no sign of how recent it is; answers I can't justify.
- **What nobody gives me today that I would want**: early, reliable notice of disruptions affecting tickets on my train, before I start checking; a clear reason with any answer; honesty about how out of date what I'm seeing is when signal is poor; a way to mark who I couldn't check and come back to them.
- **Confidence**: Medium. The disruption-notice and signal problems are what I'd expect any inspector to hit; I'm guessing about the actual tools and rules of any specific railway.
