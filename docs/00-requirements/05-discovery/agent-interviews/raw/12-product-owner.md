# Discovery interview — Product Owner, railway digital platform

Scenario probes removed; follow-ups chosen by the persona.

**Simulated by:** Claude subagent (discovery method, no system described) · **Date:** 2026-10-06 · **Persona:** Product owner accountable for value, scope and executive-facing outcomes of the railway's digital platform.

## Who I am
I'm the product owner for the railway's digital platform. I answer for what we say we will deliver and what executives see. I spend my days arbitrating between operations, passenger services and IT, and saying no. I'm impatient with feature lists; I care about the story, the riskiest assumptions and what can be cut. Everything here is simulated experience; where I'm guessing I'll say so.

## Answers

### Q1. A normal day
I start with the overnight picture: what ran late, what was cancelled, which complaints reached senior people. Then I'm in prioritisation meetings, where every group wants its item first. I spend a lot of time translating between operations people, who think in trains and shifts, and executives, who think in money, headlines and risk. I review what delivery teams have produced and decide whether it is good enough to show. Late in the day I prepare or rehearse whatever is going in front of leadership next. Most of my day is deciding what not to do.

### Q2. What breaks
Scope breaks first: it grows quietly, and each addition sounds reasonable. Next, dependencies. Something that depends on another group's data or sign-off slips, and my plan slips with it. Demos break, usually because of data that is stale or inconsistent, or because a flow works on the happy path only. When that happens I cut the scope back to what I can defend, tell sponsors early and plainly, and drop the thing that is not holding up rather than show it half working. I do not hide a failure from executives; it comes back worse.

### Q3. What information I rely on
I rely on operational performance figures from operations, incident and complaint summaries, and the delivery team's own status. I get them in reports and meetings, often a day old, and I need them before any decision that commits scope or money. I also rely on direct word from the people doing the work: drivers, control staff, station staff. I'm guessing that the most reliable signal is a frontline person telling me what they did last week, not a dashboard.

### Q4. What must never happen
Nothing may put safety at risk or mislead people about it. Executives must never see something that falls over live, or contradicts itself between screens. Personal data must never be exposed. Nobody may be told something confidently false, such as a train that is shown on time when it is not. I also never want to be surprised: bad news must reach me before it reaches a sponsor.

### Q5. If the information were confidently wrong
First I would find out how wrong, and who has already acted on it. Then I would stop what depends on it and tell the affected people directly, without waiting for a tidy explanation. Confident and wrong is worse than uncertain, because people stop checking. So I would want it clear where each figure came from and how fresh it is. After that I would look for how it got through, and I would not put that source in front of leadership again until it was fixed.

### Follow-up 1. Scope creeping in (cost: time and trust)
The last time, I'm guessing at the details, a senior sponsor asked in a corridor for "one more view" for their area. I said yes in the moment, which was my mistake. Within a week two other groups heard and asked for the same. I noticed only when the delivery lead told me the main item had slipped. I checked the plan against what we had promised, and it was about two weeks behind. I spent a day with the three requesters, and it took another week to get them to agree to drop two. It ended with the main item delivered late and one sponsor still annoyed. Now I write every request down before answering.

### Follow-up 2. A demo breaking on bad data (cost: trust and money)
The last time, I'm guessing, I saw it in the rehearsal the evening before: one screen showed a service on time while another showed it delayed. I called the delivery lead and the operations contact that night. We found the two views were using figures of different ages, a few hours apart. It took until midnight to find, and the fix was partial. We cut the second screen from the demo and told the sponsor in the morning why. The demo went ahead smaller, and the sponsor took it better than I feared because I told them first.

## Extracted by the persona
- **What I rely on today**: operations reports and meetings, incident and complaint summaries, delivery team status, frontline staff conversations.
- **What is hardest or most error-prone in my work**: holding scope against reasonable-sounding requests, especially verbal ones; spotting mismatched data ages before a demo.
- **What must never happen**: safety put at risk or misrepresented; a live failure in front of executives; contradictory information; personal data exposed; being surprised by bad news.
- **What would make me stop trusting information**: it contradicts another source, I can't tell where it came from or how old it is, or it is confident but turns out wrong.
- **What nobody gives me today that I would want**: one consistent version of events with freshness and source visible, and early warning of slippage.
